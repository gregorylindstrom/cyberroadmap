Certifications

Why Security+ is a smart first certification

Your degree shows you can learn. A certification shows an employer you have met an industry standard. For entry-level security jobs, the one employers ask for most often is CompTIA Security+.

It gets your resume past the filter

Many entry-level postings for SOC analyst, security administrator, and IT security specialist roles list Security+ as required or preferred. Some applicant tracking systems screen for it before a person ever reads your resume. Without it, you may never get a chance to explain everything else you know.

It opens government and defense work

The Department of Defense requires approved certifications for many cyber workforce roles, and Security+ is one of the most widely accepted baseline certifications. Houston has plenty of defense contractors, energy companies, and NASA suppliers that follow the same rules. For those jobs, Security+ can be the difference between getting an interview and being screened out.

It lines up with what you are already learning

Security+ covers threats, network security, identity and access, risk, and incident response. You see most of that material in your BAT courses. Studying while the content is fresh is far easier than coming back to it after graduation.

Timing note: CompTIA is releasing a new version of the exam, Security+ V8 (SY0-801), expected around November 17, 2026. If you are already studying for the current SY0-701 exam, check CompTIA's site for its retirement date and finish on the version you prepared for. If you are just starting, plan around SY0-801.

Our advice: aim to pass Security+ before you graduate, or within your first months of job searching. Ask your instructors about study resources and student exam discounts.

Career advice

Start your job search before you graduate

The most common mistake we see is waiting until after graduation to start looking. In cybersecurity, hiring often takes months, and the students who land jobs first started early.

Applications, interviews, background checks, and security clearances all take time. Many employers also fill entry-level and internship roles months in advance. If you start the day after graduation, you are already behind classmates who began a year earlier.

  1. 12+ months out
    Build a LinkedIn profile, go to your first ISACA or ISSA meeting, and start working toward Security+.
  2. 9 months out
    Update your resume with projects and labs from your BAT courses. Apply for internships, even part-time ones.
  3. 6 months out
    Start applying for full-time roles. Ask people you have met at events for informational conversations.
  4. 3 months out
    Interview, follow up, and keep applying. Do not stop because one offer looks promising.

Networking matters more than most students expect. Many jobs are filled through someone who already knows you. That is why the events section of this newsletter is there.

Role of the month

SOC Analyst

Each month we will look at one job in cybersecurity: what it involves day to day and how to get there. We start with the most common entry point into the field.

A Security Operations Center (SOC) analyst watches an organization's networks and systems for signs of an attack. When an alert fires, the analyst investigates, decides whether it is real, and either resolves it or escalates it to senior responders. Tier 1 analysts handle the first look. With experience, analysts move into incident response, threat hunting, or engineering.

A typical day
  • Review alerts in a SIEM
  • Investigate suspicious logins and traffic
  • Document findings in tickets
  • Hand off confirmed incidents
Skills to build
  • Networking and TCP/IP
  • Reading logs and packet captures
  • Windows and Linux basics
  • Clear written notes
Where you learn it here

Your networking, security, and data courses, plus Cyber Club CTFs. Security+ is a common first certification for this role.

Try it: your first alert

Try a real analyst task. Read the alert below, then write three sentences: what happened, how serious you think it is, and what you would do next.

$ siem --show alert 4471
ALERT: Multiple failed logins followed by success
user: jsmith   host: VPN-GW-01
src_ip: 203.0.113.45 (outside the US)
failed logins: 27 in 4 minutes
successful login: 02:14 CST

Save your summary. Short, clear incident write-ups like this make strong portfolio pieces and give you something concrete to talk about in interviews.

Puzzles

Crack the packet

Two challenges each month: one for anyone, one for those who want a fight. Type your answer to check it right here. Answers are verified with a SHA-256 hash, so viewing the page source will not give them away.

Level 1 · The Roman shift

Intro · about 5 min

We intercepted this message. Every letter has been shifted the same number of places through the alphabet. Find the secret word.

$ cat intercept.txt
gur frperg jbeq vf sverjnyy
Hint

Julius Caesar shifted his letters by 3. This one shifts by 13, exactly halfway around the alphabet, so doing it twice gets you back where you started.

Level 2 · Layers

Advanced · about 20 min

This one is wrapped in three layers of encoding. Peel them off in the right order to recover the flag. The answer looks like flag{...}.

$ xxd -p capture.bin
63336c756448746d5a32356c5a31396e64584a66645768685a313976636e4e695a584a6664475675635768755a335a695958303d
Hint 1

The outer layer is what you see in the bytes pane of Wireshark. Every pair of characters is one byte.

Hint 2

The middle layer ends with an equals sign once you decode the outer one. The inner layer is the same trick as Level 1. Tools like CyberChef can chain all three.
